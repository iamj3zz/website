- type: text
  title: 'What we listen for: plants as electrical bodies'
  content: |
    Long before biosonification became an artistic method, plants were already known to carry an electrical life of their own. In the early 1900s, the Bengali polymath Jagadish Chandra Bose built delicate instruments — precursors to today's biosensors — that recorded plants responding to touch, injury, temperature, and light with measurable electrical signals, arguing that plants possess a kind of nervous system responding much like our own. A century later, foresters and ecologists such as Peter Wohlleben, drawing on decades of forest research, popularized a further layer of this story: trees exchanging chemical and electrical signals through vast underground fungal networks, warning each other of drought, insects, or disease — a "wood wide web" hidden beneath every forest floor.

    The Vibrotanica boxes I build sit at the meeting point of this lineage and live performance technology. Each box reads a plant's own bioelectrical potential — the same kind of signal Bose first measured — and streams it, in real time, into sound and generative visuals. Nothing is added, dramatized, or invented: the plant's own fluctuating voltage becomes a melody, a texture, a shifting image on screen. What workshop participants hear and see is the plant's own signal, translated rather than composed.
- type: text
  title: Experiments in listening
  content: |
    During each session, small groups adopt a plant for the length of the workshop and simply listen. We try things, together, and see what happens — or doesn't:

    - Watering a thirsty plant and following the sonified signal as it shifts, or stays remarkably still.
    - A gentle touch on a leaf, echoing Bose's century-old experiments — does the sound change? Does it settle back to where it was, and how quickly?
    - Comparing two plants of the same species, side by side, to notice how much individual variation exists even under identical conditions.
    - Moving a plant from shade into light, or exposing it to music or vibration, and listening for a response — or for silence, which is itself a result worth sitting with.
    - Returning to the same plant at different times of day, to hear whether its voice changes with the light, the temperature, or the presence of people in the room.

    We treat an absence of change with the same curiosity as a dramatic one. Not every gesture provokes a response, and part of what the workshop teaches — gently, without lecturing — is the discipline of observing without forcing a story onto what we hear.
- type: text
  title: A room shared across generations
  content: |
    Biosonification workshops are deliberately built as intergenerational, cross-community spaces. In a single session, a retired grower who has kept the same fig tree for thirty years might sit beside a ten-year-old hearing a plant's "voice" for the first time, next to a biology researcher, a hobbyist gardener, an artist, or someone who has simply never thought about plants at all. Nobody arrives more qualified to listen than anyone else — the plant's signal doesn't care about anyone's expertise.

    What emerges from these sessions is less a demonstration than a shared inquiry: growers bring decades of hands-on plant knowledge, scientists bring rigor and healthy skepticism, children bring unguarded attention, and everyone leaves having listened, together, to something usually silent. This is the community that "Nout plant i chant" was built around — plant lovers, growers, families, and researchers meeting on equal footing around the same quiet, electrical hum of a living plant.
