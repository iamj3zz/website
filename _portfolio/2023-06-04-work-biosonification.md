- type: text
  title: 'What we listen for: plants as electrical bodies'
  content: |
    Long before biosonification became an artistic method, plants were already known to carry an electrical life of their own. In the early 1900s, the Bengali polymath Jagadish Chandra Bose built delicate instruments — precursors to today's biosensors — that recorded plants responding to touch, injury, temperature, and light with measurable electrical signals, arguing that plants possess a kind of nervous system responding much like our own. A century later, foresters and ecologists such as Peter Wohlleben, drawing on decades of forest research, popularized a further layer of this story: trees exchanging chemical and electrical signals through vast underground fungal networks, warning each other of drought, insects, or disease — a "wood wide web" hidden beneath every forest floor.

    More recently, researchers have shown that stressed plants — dehydrated, cut, or otherwise under duress — emit airborne ultrasonic clicks: sounds far above the range of human hearing, audible to us only through sensitive microphones and specialized recording equipment, and detected in the wild by moths and other animals able to hear them. It's a striking finding, one more proof that plants are far from silent, even when their sound-making stays hidden without the right instruments.

    I chose a different path with Vibrotanica. Rather than recording a plant's rare, stress-triggered airborne clicks, each box reads its ongoing bioelectrical potential directly — the same kind of electrical signal Bose first measured — and streams it, live, into sound and generative visuals that anyone in the room can hear and see, no ultrasonic microphone or laboratory conditions required. It's a continuous signal rather than an alarm, translated rather than composed, and audible the moment you walk into the room.
- type: text
  title: Experiments in listening
  content: |
    During each session, small groups adopt a plant for the length of the workshop and simply listen. We try things, together, and see what happens — or doesn't:

    - Watering a thirsty plant and following the sonified signal as it shifts, or stays remarkably still.
    - A gentle touch on a leaf, echoing Bose's century-old experiments — does the sound change? Does it settle back to where it was, and how quickly?
    - Comparing two plants of the same species, side by side, to notice how much individual variation exists even under identical conditions.
    - Moving a plant from shade into light, or exposing it to music or vibration, and listening for a response — or for silence, which is itself a result worth sitting with.
    - Returning to the same plant at different times of day, to hear whether its voice changes with the light, the temperature, or the presence of people in the room.

    We treat an absence of change with the same curiosity as a dramatic one. Not every gesture provokes a response, and part of what the workshop teaches — gently, without lecturing — is the discipline of observing without forcing a story onto what we hear.
- type: text
  title: A room shared across generations
  content: |
    The reactions in the room are part of what makes this work meaningful. Children, often the quickest to let go of self-consciousness, lean in close to the speaker, ask if the plant is "talking" or "singing," and start proposing their own experiments before we've finished explaining the setup. Older participants — many of whom have spent a lifetime tending gardens, orchards, or houseplants — tend to listen differently: less surprised that a plant might have something to express, more curious about which plant, in which condition, produces which sound. Between these two ways of listening lies something universal: everyone carries some relationship to the plant world, whether it's a childhood memory of a garden, a windowsill of pots kept alive through effort, or simply the plants we walk past every day without registering them as anything but background. Biosonification interrupts that background noise and gives it a voice.

    Biosonification workshops are deliberately built as intergenerational, cross-community spaces. In a single session, a retired grower who has kept the same fig tree for thirty years might sit beside a ten-year-old hearing a plant's "voice" for the first time, next to a biology researcher, a hobbyist gardener, an artist, or someone who has simply never thought about plants at all. Nobody arrives more qualified to listen than anyone else — the plant's signal doesn't care about anyone's expertise.

    What emerges from these sessions is less a demonstration than a shared inquiry: growers bring decades of hands-on plant knowledge, scientists bring rigor and healthy skepticism, children bring unguarded attention, and everyone leaves having listened, together, to something usually silent. This is the community that "Nout plant i chant" was built around — plant lovers, growers, families, and researchers meeting on equal footing around the same quiet, electrical hum of a living plant.
- type: text
  title: A collective memory we all seem to carry
  content: |
    Something unplanned happens in almost every session: once people hear a plant's electrical signal turned into sound, they start telling stories. A participant remembers a grandmother who sang to her tomato plants every morning. Someone else recalls being told, as a child, to talk gently to the ferns so they wouldn't wilt. Someone from a different country entirely describes an aunt who swore her orchids only bloomed when spoken to in a particular tone of voice. These stories arrive unprompted, and they arrive from everywhere — every country, every culture represented in the room seems to carry some version of the same instinct: that plants are listening, in some sense, and that speaking or singing to them matters.

    Biosonification doesn't set out to prove or disprove any of this. What it does is give shape to an intuition that already exists, quietly, in nearly every culture's relationship to the plants people keep close — on windowsills, in gardens, passed down through generations of gesture and habit rather than instruction. Hearing a plant's own signal turned into sound seems to unlock permission to say these stories out loud, often for the first time in a formal setting, and to recognize that this instinct to speak to something growing is closer to universal than anyone expected.
- type: text
  title: 'Beyond sound: scent, chemistry, and emotion'
  content: |
    Electrical signals and airborne clicks are not the only language plants speak. Many of the same plants used in these workshops also produce essential oils — aromatic compounds that plants use to defend themselves, deter pests, or attract pollinators, and that humans have used for centuries, through aromatherapy and traditional plant medicine, to influence mood and emotional state. A sprig of lavender, a leaf of rosemary crushed between the fingers, releases a scent long associated with calm or alertness, long before we had any electrical instrument to measure it.

    Bringing this up in a workshop built around bioelectric sound tends to open a further layer of awareness: that our relationship to plants runs through more channels than we usually notice — electrical, acoustic, chemical, and emotional all at once. Participants often leave curious not only about what a plant sounds like, but about what it smells like, what it once meant to a grandmother's kitchen or a grandfather's garden, and how much of that inherited, often unspoken knowledge about scent and feeling deserves the same attention we're learning to give its electrical voice.
- type: text
  title: A poetic reconnection
  content: |
    Underneath the science and the sensors, biosonification is, first and foremost, a poetic experience. It doesn't aim to prove that plants think or feel the way we do — it aims to give us, for the length of a workshop, a reason to stand still in front of something growing and pay attention to it as if it might be saying something. In a life increasingly lived indoors, at screens, at a remove from soil and season, that pause is its own quiet form of reconnection: to the plants themselves, to the people standing beside us listening to the same sound, and to something older in ourselves that never really stopped believing a garden might answer back.
